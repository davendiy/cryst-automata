\begin{abstract}
  A group $G$ admits self-replicating action of degree $d$ if it acts on a space of words over alphabet of size $d$.
  A group $G$ admits .... if and only if it has a subgroup $H$ of index $d$ and a homomorphism
  $\phi : H \rightarrow G$ with trivial core. Every crystallographic group admits a self-replicating action.
  We determine self-replicating degrees for crystallographic groups of dimension $n=2,3$.
\end{abstract}

Crystallographic groups describe symmetries of repeated patterns of an $n$-dimensional space. They were known for
several centuries in case of $n=2$, though the complete list was only given in 1891 by Fedorov. In general, they appeared
as a part of 18th Hilbert's problem, and were characterized by Bieberbach and Zassenhaus later on. There are 17 distinct
groups for $n=2$ up to isometry, known as wallpaper groups or plane groups. Likewise, in $n=3$ there are 219 crystallographic groups,
known as space groups. If, however, space groups are considered up to conjugacy with respect
to orientation-preserving affine transformations, the number is increased to 230.

Self-similar and self-replicating actions describe the recursive patterns of groups acting on words over the given finite
alphabet. They can also be viewed as actions on a rooted tree or as a result of the work of a finite transducer. It was
studied by Grigorchuk, Nekrashevych, Sidki, etc, and has great impact on dynamical systems, \textbf{TODO} bla bla bla.

In this paper we focus mainly on self-replicating actions of crystallographic groups, particularly on sizes of the
respective alphabets that occur therein. A group has a self-replicating degree $d$, if it admits a self-replicating
action over an alphabet of size $d$. We, however, will use another definition, which does not depend on actions and
uses a toolkit of virtual endomorphisms developed by Nekrashevych \cite{Nekrashevych:Self-similar}.
\begin{definition}
  A virtual endomophism $\phi : H \rightarrow G$ of a group $G$ is a homomorphism from
  its subgroup of finite index $H$. A virtual endomorphism $\phi$ is called \textit{simple}, if there is no nontrivial
  $\phi$-invariant normal subgroup of $G$.
\end{definition}

A group is \textit{self-similar} if there exists a simple virtual endomorphism from its subgroup of index $d$.
If it is also surjective, then we say the group is \textit{self-replicating} and
has a \textit{self-replicating degree} $d$. This concept will be the main object of our studying. It is closely
related to the following one: a group has a \textit{self-covering degree} $d$ if it has an isomorphic subgroup of
index $d$. Self-similar groups can be viewed as the iterated monodromy groups of a self-covering map of the complex
plane. % TODO: more details of self-covering

Virtual endomorphisms were used to analyze self-similarity for a wide range of groups: free abelian groups,
finitely generated nilpotent groups, solvable groups, wreath products of abelian groups and so on.
Despite every virtual abelian group is self-similar (\cite{Abelian}), and therefore every crystallographic as well,
not every one is self-replicating. As an example, consider $G = \mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}$ as a counterexample
due to \cite{Sidki}.

\section{Crystallographic groups}\label{sec:cryst}

We briefly recall the necessary background on crystallographic groups; a detailed exposition can be found in \cite{Szcz:CrystBook}.

Let $\mathbf{A}(n)$ denote the group of affine transformations of $\mathbb{R}^n$, and let $\mathbf{E}(n)\le \mathbf{A}(n)$ denote the group of Euclidean isometries. The group $\mathbf{A}(n)$ admits the decomposition
$\mathbf{A}(n)=GL_n(\mathbb{R})\ltimes \mathbb{R}^n$, and every element can be written in the form $(A,a)$, where $A\in GL_n(\mathbb{R})$ and $a\in\mathbb{R}^n$. The group operation is given by
\[
(A,a)(B,b)=(AB,Ab+a).
\]

\begin{definition}
A \emph{crystallographic group} of dimension $n$ is a discrete cocompact subgroup of $\mathbf{E}(n)$. Crystallographic groups of dimension $2$ are called \emph{planar groups}, and those of dimension $3$ are called \emph{space groups}.
\end{definition}

The algebraic structure of crystallographic groups is described by the following classical theorems of Bieberbach.

\begin{theorem}[Bieberbach]
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Let $G\le\mathbf{E}(n)$ be a crystallographic group. Then the subgroup of translations
  $G \cap (E \times \mathbb{R}^n)$ is a free abelian group of rank $n$, and it
  is a maximal abelian normal subgroup of finite index in $G$.

  \item Two crystallographic groups of dimension $n$ are isomorphic if and only if they are conjugate
  in the affine group $\mathbf{A}(n)$.

  \item For every $n\in\mathbb{N}$, there exist only finitely many isomorphism classes of crystallographic groups of dimension $n$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

For a crystallographic group $G$, its \emph{translation subgroup} is $T(G)=G \cap (E \times \mathbb{R}^n)$, and the corresponding subgroup $L(G)=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^n : (E,v)\in T(G)\}\cong T(G)$ is called the \emph{translation lattice} of $G$. The \emph{point group} $P(G)$ is the image of $G$ under the natural projection onto $GL_n(\mathbb{R})$.
By the Bieberbach theorems, there is a short exact sequence
\[
0 \rightarrow L \rightarrow G \rightarrow P \rightarrow 0,
\]
where $L=L(G)\cong\mathbb{Z}^n$ and $P=P(G)$ is a finite subgroup of $GL_n(\mathbb{R})$. 

Conversely, given a finite subgroup $P\le GL_n(\mathbb{R})$ and a $P$-invariant lattice $L\le\mathbb{R}^n$, the crystallographic groups fitting this sequence are classified by the cohomology group
$H^1(P, \mathbb{R}^n / L) \cong H^2(P, L)$. Thus, an arbitrary crystallographic group can be represented as a triple
$G = (P, L, \alpha)$, where $\alpha \in H^1(P, \mathbb{R}^n / L)$, that is, $\alpha : P \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^n / L$ satisfies $\alpha(AB) = \alpha(A) + A\alpha(B)$. Two cocycles $\alpha$ and
$\alpha'$ represent the same cohomology class if they differ by a $1$-coboundary, meaning $\alpha'(A) = \alpha(A) + Am - m$ for some $m \in \mathbb{R}^n / L$. Choosing representatives of $\alpha(A)$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ for $A\in P$, the elements of $G$ admit explicit representation:
$$
G = \{ (A, \alpha(A) + t) \,|\, A \in P, \, t \in L \}.
$$
If $\alpha$ is trivial, i.e.
$\alpha(A) = 0$ for every $A \in P$, the group $G$ is isomorphic to the semidirect product $P \ltimes L$ and is
called \textit{symmorphic}.
More details on group cohomology and its applications to crystallographic groups can be found in \cite{Szcz:CrystBook,Brown:Cohomology}.

The following properties of crystallographic groups will be used in what follows.

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:norm_cryst}
Every normal subgroup of a crystallographic group is either trivial or infinite.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:fisubgroup_cryst}
Every finite-index subgroup of a crystallographic group is crystallographic.
\end{lemma}


\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:equal_index}
Let $G$ be a crystallographic group and $H\le G$ be a subgroup isomorphic to $G$.
Then $T(H)=T(G)\cap H$ and $[G:H] = [T(G):T(H)]$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The equality $T(H)=H\cap T(G)$ follows directly from the definition of the translation subgroup. Since $G$ and $H$ are isomorphic, the Bieberbach theorem implies that $P(G)\cong P(H)$. On the other hand, $P(H)\le P(G)$ and hence $P(H)=P(G)$. Then $[H:T(H)]=|P(H)|=|P(G)|=[G:T(G)]$. The index of $T(H)$ in $G$ can be expressed in two ways as
\[
[G:T(H)]=[G:H][H:T(H)]=[G:T(G)][T(G):T(H)].
\]
The equality $[G:H] = [T(G):T(H)]$ follows.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{th:surjection-is-bijection}
Let $G$ be a crystallographic group, $H\le G$ a subgroup of finite index, and $\phi:H\rightarrow G$ a surjective homomorphism. Then $\phi$ is an isomorphism.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let $h(\cdot)$ denote the Hirsch length of a group, and let $K$ be the kernel of $\phi$. Since $\phi$ is surjective, $h(H)=h(G)+h(K)$. As $H\le G$ has finite index, $h(H)=h(G)$. Hence, $h(K)=0$, and therefore $K$ is finite. Since $K$ is a normal subgroup of $H$ and $H$ is crystallographic by Lemma~\ref{lemma:fisubgroup_cryst}, Lemma~\ref{lemma:norm_cryst} implies $K$ is trivial. Hence $\phi$ is injective, and therefore an isomorphism.
\end{proof}

By the Bieberbach theorem and Lemma~\ref{th:surjection-is-bijection},
every surjective virtual endomorphism $\phi: H \rightarrow G$ of a crystallographic group $G$ is induced by conjugation with an affine element $(A, t) \in \mathbf{A}(n)$:
\[
\phi(g) = (A, t)^{-1}g(A, t)  \ \mbox{ for } \ h\in H = (A, t)G(A, t)^{-1}.
\] 
The action of $\phi$ on the translation subgroup $T(H)$ is determined by the linear part $A$:
\[
\phi(E,v)=(A, t)^{-1}(E,v)(A, t)=(E,A^{-1}v) \ \mbox{ for } \ (E,v)\in H.
\]
It follows that $L(H) = AL(G)\le L(G)$, and by Lemma~\ref{lemma:equal_index},
\[
[G:H]=[T(G):T(H)]=[L(G):L(H)]=[L(G):AL(G)]=|\det(A)|.
\]
Therefore, the set of self-covering degrees coincides with the set of absolute values of determinants of matrices occurring as linear parts of surjective virtual endomorphisms. To determine the self-replicating degrees, it remains to analyze the simplicity condition.

%We next characterize simple virtual endomorphisms for crystallographic groups with translation lattice $\mathbb{Z}^n$. 
\begin{theorem}\label{th:simpe_virt}
Let $G\le\mathbf{A}(n)$ be a crystallographic group, and let $a = (A,t)\in \mathbf{A}(n)$ be such that $aGa^{-1}\le G$. Consider the virtual endomorphism $\phi: H=aGa^{-1}\rightarrow G$ defined by $\phi(g) = a^{-1}ga$. Then $\phi$ is simple if and only if no eigenvalue of $A^{-1}$ is an algebraic integer (equivalently, the characteristic polynomial of $A^{-1}$ is not divisible by a non-constant monic polynomial with integral coefficients).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
We may assume that the translation lattice of $G$ is $\mathbb{Z}^n$.
Since $aGa^{-1}\le G$, we have $A\mathbb{Z}^n\leq\mathbb{Z}^n$, and hence $A$ has integer entries. Consider the virtual endomorphism $\psi:A\mathbb{Z}^n\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^n$, $\psi(v)=A^{-1}v$, of the group $\mathbb{Z}^n$, which is the restriction of $\phi$ to the translation lattice. By Proposition~2.9.1 of \cite{Nekrashevych:Self-similar}, the maximal $\psi$-invariant subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}^n$ is given by
\[
K(\psi) = \bigcap_{m=1}^{\infty} A^m \mathbb{Z}^n.
\]
By Proposition~2.9.2 of \cite{Nekrashevych:Self-similar}, $K(\psi)=\{0\}$ if and only if
$A^{-1}$ has no eigenvalues that are algebraic integers. It remains to show that $\phi$ is simple if and only if $K(\psi)=\{0\}$.

Assume that $K(\psi)\neq\{0\}$. Since $aGa^{-1}\leq G$, the linear part $A$ commutes with the point group $P$ of $G$, that is, $AP=PA$. Hence each subgroup $A^m\mathbb{Z}^n$ is $P$-invariant, and therefore so is $K(\psi)$. Since $P$-invariant subgroups of the translation lattice give rise to normal subgroups of $G$, the subgroup $E\times K(\psi)$ is a nontrivial  normal subgroups of $G$. Moreover, $E\times K(\psi)$ is $\phi$-invariant by construction. Therefore, $\phi$ is not simple.

Conversely, let $K$ be a nontrivial normal $\phi$-invariant subgroup of $G$. By Lemma~\ref{lemma:fisubgroup_cryst}, $K$ is infinite and therefore contains a nontrivial translation. Let $m=\operatorname{lcm}\{\operatorname{ord}(B) : B\in P\}$. Then $g^{m}\in T(G)$ for every $g\in G$. Consider the subgroup $K_m=\langle g^{m}, g\in K\rangle\le T(G)$. Since $K$ is nontrivial, normal in $G$, and $\phi$-invariant, it follows that $K_m$ is also nontrivial, normal in $G$, and $\phi$-invariant. Identifying $K_m$ with the corresponding subgroup of the translation lattice, we obtain a nontrivial $\psi$-invariant subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}^n$. Hence
$K_m\le K(\psi)$, and therefore $K(\psi)\neq{0}$, which completes the proof.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
An integer $d \ge 2$ is a self-replicating degree of a crystallographic group $G\le \mathbf{A}(n)$ if and only if
there exists $a=(A,t)\in\mathbf{A}(n)$ with $|\det(A)| = d$ such that $aGa^{-1} \le G$ and
no eigenvalue of $A^{-1}$ is an algebraic integer.
\end{corollary}


We now obtain an upper bound for self-replicating degrees of crystallographic groups.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:upper_bound}
Let $G$ be a crystallographic group of dimension $n$ with point group $P$. Then $G$ has a self-replicating degree at most $(|P|+1)^n$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
We may assume that the translation lattice of $G$ is $\mathbb{Z}^n$.
Let $m$ be any positive integer that annihilates the defining 1-cocycle $[\alpha] \in H^1(P, \mathbb{R}^n/\mathbb{Z}^n)$ of $G$, meaning $mv \in \mathbb{Z}^n$ for all $(B,v) \in G$. Note that $m = |P|$ always satisfies this property by standard group cohomology (see \cite{Brown:Cohomology}).

Define the scalar matrix $A = (m+1)E$ and the affine transformation $a = (A, 0)$. Conjugating an arbitrary element $(B, v) \in G$ by $a$ gives:
$$ a (B, v) a^{-1} = (B, Av) = (B, (m+1)v) = (B, v)(E, mv) $$.
Since $mv \in \mathbb{Z}^n$, the term $(E, mv)$ is a lattice translation, implying $aGa^{-1} \le G$. The unique eigenvalue of $A^{-1}$ is $\frac{1}{m+1}$, which is not an algebraic integer, making $\det(A) = (m+1)^n$ a valid self-replicating degree.
\end{proof}

  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Every symmorphic crystallographic group of dimension $n$ possesses a self-replicating degree at most $2^n$.
    \item Every planar group possesses a self-replicating degree at most $9$.
    \item Every space group possesses a self-replicating degree at most $343$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
For a symmorphic crystallographic group, the defining cocycle is trivial. Hence the proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:upper_bound} applies with $m=1$, yielding a self-replicating degree at most $2^n$.

For planar groups, the defining cocycle is annihilated by $m=2$, yielding a degree at most $(2+1)^2 = 9$. For space groups, the cocycle is annihilated by $m=6$, yielding a degree at most $(6+1)^3 = 343$.
\end{proof}

\section{Computing self-replicating degrees of crystallographic groups}\label{sec:computations}

Let $G$ be a crystallographic group. We consider two sets of positive integers associated with $G$:
\begin{align*}
SCD(G) &= \{d \in \mathbb{N} \, | \, \text{ $G$ has a subgroup $H$ of index $d$ isomorphic to $G$ } \},\\
SRD(G) &= \{d \in \mathbb{N}\, | \, \text{ $G$ admits a simple surjective virtual
                                               endomorphism from subgroup of index $d$ } \}.
\end{align*}
It is clear that $SRD\subset SCD$.
We denote by $MinSCD(G)$ and $MinSRD(G)$ as the minimal self-covering and self-replicating degrees.
From a computational perspective, both $SCD(G)$ and $SRD(G)$ can be viewed as decision problems for the group $G$.

\vspace{0.2cm}

\subsection{Computing self-covering degrees.}
Let us describe a method for computing the set $SCD(G)$ for a given crystallographic group $G=(P,\mathbb{Z}^n,\alpha)$.
A positive integer $d$ belongs to $SCD(G)$ if and only if there exists an affine transformation
$a=(A,t)\in Aff(\mathbb{Q})$ such that $d=|\det A|$ and $a G a^{-1}\leq G$. This condition splits into three parts:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[1)] Point group compatibility: $APA^{-1}=P$.
  \item[2)] Lattice compatibility: $A\mathbb{Z}^n\leq \mathbb{Z}^n$, which means $A$ must have integer entries.
  \item[3)] Cocycle compatibility: for every $B\in P$,
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:cocycle_compatibility}
\alpha(ABA^{-1})\equiv(E-ABA^{-1})t+A\alpha(B)  \mod \mathbb{Z}^n.
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
To determine possible values of $|\det A|$, we first describe the integer matrices $A$ satisfying the first condition
and then identify those for which there exists a translation $t$ satisfying the third condition. It is clear that
for symmorphic groups the third condition is trivial.

The integer matrices $A$ satisfying the first condition can be described as follows.
Let $\sigma \in \mathrm{Aut}(P)$ be an automorphism of the point group.
Consider the system of linear equations over the integers
\[
X B = \sigma(B) X \quad \mbox{ for all $B \in P$},
\]
where $X \in M_{n}(\mathbb{Z})$ is the unknown matrix. The set $\mathcal{X}_\sigma$ of all
solutions is a finitely generated $\mathbb{Z}$-module, and there exists a finite, computable set of integral
matrices $X_1, \dots, X_m \in \mathcal{X}_\sigma$ such that every $X \in \mathcal{X}_\sigma$ can be written as
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:X_sum_Xi}
X = \sum_{i=1}^{m} x_i X_i, \quad x_i \in \mathbb{Z}.
\end{equation}
The total set $\mathcal{X}$ of matrices $A$ satisfying 1) is the union of $\mathcal{X}_\sigma$
over all $\sigma \in \mathrm{Aut}(P)$.

Next, we determine those matrices $X\in \mathcal{X}$ for which a translation $t$ exists satisfying the cocycle
compatibility condition. Let $\sigma \in \mathrm{Aut}(P)$ and $X\in \mathcal{X}_\sigma$ be written as the linear
combination (\ref{eqn:X_sum_Xi}) with unknown $x_i\in\mathbb{Z}$. The equation (\ref{eqn:cocycle_compatibility})
for the matrix $X$ can be written as a system of congruence: given fixed unknown coefficients
$x_i\in\mathbb{Z}$ and the translation vector $t\in\mathbb{Q}^n$ for all $B\in P$ the following is true
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:cocycle_condition}
  \sum_{i=1}^{m} x_i X_i \alpha(B) - \alpha(\sigma(B)) \equiv (\sigma(B) - E)t  \mod \mathbb{Z}^n.
\end{equation}
Denote left part of the (\ref{eqn:cocycle_condition}) as an unknown rational vector $b_B(x_1, \dots x_n) \in \mathbb{Q}^n$ and
the right part $(\sigma(B) - E)$ as an integer matrix $M_B$. Combining for every $B \in P$ we get an auxiliary
linear system:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:rational_t}
  Mt \equiv b \mod \mathbb{Z}^n
\end{equation}
where $M = \begin{pmatrix} \dots \\ M_{B} \\ \dots \end{pmatrix}$ and
$b = \begin{pmatrix} \dots \\ b_{B} \\ \dots \end{pmatrix}$ are the large matrix and vector consisting
of stacked $M_B$ and $b_B$ respectively for every $B$ from the point group.

\begin{proposition}
  The congruence system (\ref{eqn:rational_t}) is solvable in $t \in \mathbb{Q}^n$ if and only if
  for every integral $v \in \mathbb{Z}^n$ from $v^\top M = 0$ follows that $v^\top b \in \mathbb{Z}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  $(\Rightarrow)$ If $Mt = b + z$ for some rational $t$ and integral $z$, then for every integral vector
  $v$ we can write $v^\top Mt= v^\top b+v^\top z$, which proves the statement.

  $(\Leftarrow)$
  Let us write the Smith Normal Form for $M$ as a matrix over the principal ideal domain $\mathbb{Z}$:
  $UMV=D$, where $D$ is a diagonal matrix of rank $r$ and $U,V$ are invertible square integral matrices.
  Then $Mt-b \in Z^n$ if and only if
  $U(Mt-b) \in Z^n$. Rewrite it as $U(Mt-b) = UMV t'-b'$, where $t'=V^{-1} t$ and $b'= Ub$.

  Now $Dt'-b' \in Z^n$ for some rational $t'$ if and only if for every zero diagonal element of $D$ with index
  $i>r$ the respective vector's entity $b'_i \in Z^n$. Returning to the original notation $b'_i= u_ib$, where $u_i$ is
  an integral row vector of $U$ we observe that $u_i M = 0$ for $i>r$. This, combined with the assumed
  statement, leads to $b'_i = u_i b \in Z$.
\end{proof}

Denote $u_i(M)$ as the i-th row of the matrix $U$ in the Smith decomposition $UMV = D$ over the ring $\mathbb{Z}$.
The proof allows us to rewrite the proposition and focus on the $u_i$, instead of checking the statement
for every integral vector.

\begin{corollary}
  The system $Mt \equiv b \mod \mathbb{Z}^n$ has a rational solution $t$ if and only if
  for every $i > r$ $u_i(M)b\in \mathbb{Z}$, where $r$ is the rank of $M$.
\end{corollary}

This gives us a constructive way to eliminate the rational $t$ from the system (\ref{eqn:cocycle_condition})
and instead consider a finite system of linear congruences in the integral indeterminates $x_i$:
for $j > \text{rank}(M)$ and $b(x_1, \dots, x_m)$ be a stacked column vector of
$b_B(x_1, \dots x_m) = \sum_{i=1}^{m} x_i X_i \alpha(B) - \alpha(\sigma(B))$:
\[
  u_j(M)b(x_1, \dots, x_m) \equiv 0 \mod \ 1
\]
Solving this system in general form and substituting the solution into $X$, we obtain a finite, computable set of
integral matrices $Y_0, Y_1, \dots, Y_r$. The subset
$\mathcal{Y}_\sigma\subseteq \mathcal{X}_\sigma$ consisting of all matrices satisfying conditions 1)--3) is then given by
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:Y_sum_Yi}
  Y = Y_0+\sum_{i=1}^{r} y_i Y_i, \quad y_i \in \mathbb{Z}.
\end{equation}
The determinant of $Y$ can be expressed as an integer polynomial $f_\sigma(y_1,\ldots,y_r)$.
Denote $\mathcal{Y}$ as the union of $\mathcal{Y}_\sigma$ over $\sigma \in \mathrm{Aut}(P)$.

It is worth noting that solving a rational system of the linear congruences over the integers requires careful approach.
It also appears as an auxiliary problem on a way to classify crystallographic groups up to isometry, particularly
during calculation $H^1(P, \mathbb{R}^n/L)$. One might find more details on this topic in \cite{Algo:Cocycle}.

Finally, we get the following description of the set of self-covering and self-replicating degrees:
\begin{align*}
  SCD(G)&=\{ |\det A|\neq 0 : A\in \mathcal{Y}\}=\{ |f_\sigma(y_1,\ldots, y_r)|\neq 0 : y_i\in\mathbb{Z} \mbox{ and }
  \sigma\in Aut(P) \},\\
  SRD(G)&=\{ |\det A|\neq 0 : A\in \mathcal{Y} \mbox{ and no eigenvalue of $A^{-1}$ is an algebraic integer}\}.
\end{align*}
The set of integer values of an integer polynomial generally admits no simple or computable arithmetic description.
Determining whether a given integer $d>0$ belongs to $SCD(G)$ amounts to deciding the solvability of the Diophantine
equation $|f_\sigma(y_1,\ldots, y_r)|=d$ for some $\sigma\in Aut(P)$. By Matiyasevich's theorem, such problems are
undecidable for arbitrary polynomials. We do not know whether the problem is decidable in our settings:

\begin{problem}
  Let $G$ be a crystallographic group.

  (1) Is it decidable, for a given integer $d$, whether there exists a subgroup $H<G$ of index $d$ with $H\cong G$?
   Equivalently, does the orbifold $\mathbb{R}^n/G$ admit a self-covering of degree $d$?

  (2) Is it decidable, for a given integer $d$, whether the group $G$ has a self-replicating degree $d$? Equivalently,
  does $G$ admit a self-replicating action over the alphabet of size $d$?
\end{problem}

\subsection{Planar groups}\label{subsec:planar}
In this section we show how to describe the sets $SCD(G)$ and $SRD(G)$ for crystallographic groups in the dimension two.
A determinant of the unknown $2\times2$ matrix $f_\sigma$ is a quadratic form of $4$ or less variables and, in general
case, is a complex object to determine values over integrals. The explicit computation of matrices $\mathcal{X}$
satisfying condition 1) reveals that for the most planar groups the determinant $f_\sigma$ is a quadratic polynomial
of 1 or 2 variables (Appendix \ref{app:all_norm_dim2}). The exception, however, are groups p1 (No.1) and p2 (No.2),
whose point groups commute with every possible $2\times2$ matrix and have trivial cocycle condition. For these groups
$SCD$ and $SRD$ contain every positive integer greater than 1, which will be shown later.

We reduce the condition on the eigenvalues in general case to a simpler arithmetic condition:

\begin{proposition}\label{propos:planar-srdegrees}
  Let $A\in M_2(\mathbb{Z})$ and $\det(A)\neq 0$. No eigenvalue of $A^{-1}$ is an algebraic integer if and only if
   $\det(A)\neq \pm 1$ and $\det(A) \pm \operatorname{tr}(A) \neq -1$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
  The characteristic polynomials of $A$ and $A^{-1}$ are
  \begin{align*}
  \chi_A(x)&=x^2-\tr(A) x + \det(A)\in\mathbb{Z}[x],\\
  \chi_{A^{-1}}(x)&=x^2-\frac{\tr(A)}{\det(A)} x + \frac{1}{\det(A)}\in\mathbb{Q}[x].
  \end{align*}
  If $\det(A)=\pm 1$, then $\chi_{A^{-1}}(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]$ and its roots are algebraic integers. If $\det(A) \pm \tr(A) = -1$, then $A^{-1}$ has an eigenvalue $1$ or $-1$.

  Conversely, assume $\chi_{A^{-1}}(x)$ is divisible by a monic polynomial $p(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]$. If $\deg p(x)=2$,
  then $p(x)=\chi_{A^{-1}}(x)$ and $\det(A)=\pm 1$. If $\deg p(x)=1$ and $p(x)=x-a$, then $a$ is an integer eigenvalue
  of $A^{-1}$. Now $a^{-1}$ is a root of a monic integer polynomial $\chi_A(x)$. Since $a$ and $a^{-1}$ are both
  algebraic integer, we have $a=\pm 1$ and
  \[
  \chi_{A}(\pm 1)=1\mp \tr(A)+\det(A)=0 \quad \Rightarrow  \quad \det(A)\mp \tr(A)=-1.
  \]
\end{proof}

The trace of a matrix $Y\in \mathcal{Y}_\sigma$ written in the form (\ref{eqn:Y_sum_Yi}) can be expressed by the
linear combination $g_\sigma(y_1,\ldots,y_r)$. This leads us to the following characterization of $SRD(G)$:
\begin{align*}
  SRD(G) = \big\{ | f_\sigma(y_1,\ldots, y_r)|>1 \ : & \ \sigma\in Aut(P) \text{ and } y_i\in\mathbb{Z} \text{ such that } \\
  & \quad ( f_\sigma \pm g_\sigma)(y_1,\ldots,y_r)\neq -1 \big\}.
\end{align*}
For the case p1 and p2, the condition is the widest:
\[
  SRD(G_{1,2}) = \big\{ | y_1y_4 - y_2y_3 |>1 \ :  \  y_i\in\mathbb{Z} \text{ and } y_1y_4 - y_2y_3 \pm (y_1 + y_4) \neq -1 \big\}.
\]
It is easy to show that it represents every positive integer setting $y_1=y_4=0,\  y_2=n>1, \ y_3=1$, i.e. for
the matrices of a kind $\begin{pmatrix}0 & n \\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$.

For the remaining 15 planar groups the calculated matrices $\mathcal{Y}$ satisfying conditions 1)-3) 
are given in the Appendix \ref{app:all_norm_dim2}. The generators and naming conventions are taken from the
International Tables for Crystallography \cite{CrystTables}. As it was already mentioned, the polynomials $f_\sigma$
and $g_\sigma$ contain at most 2 variables, so the condition $f_\sigma \pm g_\sigma \neq -1$ can be solved automatically
\cite{quadratic_polys}. Consequently, the Table \ref{tab:planar_srdegrees} presents the computed polynomials
$f_\sigma$ for every planar group --- effectively the description of $SCD$ --- and the computed solutions for
$f_\sigma \pm g_\sigma \neq -1$, which combined with $f_\sigma$ is the description of $SRD$. Additionaly,
the $MinSCD$ and $MinSRD$ are computed and are provided in the Table \ref{tab:min_srdegrees}.

\subsection{Space Groups}\label{subsec:space}
A similar approach for describing the sets $SCD$ and $SRD$ is possible for dimension 3. Although every polynomial
$f_\sigma$ has degree 3 and can not be solved in general case, the explicit computation of matrices $\mathcal{X}$
similarly restricts the amount of free variables. Moreover, we can reduce the condition on the eigenvalues
to the two-dimensional case and the Proposition \ref{propos:planar-srdegrees}.

Let $A$ be an arbitrary $3\times 3$ integral matrix.
$$
  A = \left(\begin{array}{rrr}
  x_{0} & x_{1} & x_{2} \\
  x_{3} & x_{4} & x_{5} \\
  x_{6} & x_{7} & x_{8}
  \end{array}\right)
$$
Its characteristic polynomial is written as:
$$
  \chi_A(x) = x^3 -\tr(A)x^2 + \tau(A)x + \det(A).
$$
$$
  \chi_{A^{-1}}(x)= x^3 - \frac{\tau(A)}{\det(A)}x^2 + \frac{\tr(A)}{\det(A)}x + \frac{1}{\det(A)}.
$$
where $\tau(A) := (-x_1x_3 + x_0x_4 - x_2x_6 - x_5x_7 + x_0x_8 + x_4x_8)$.

Let $G$ be a space group with a point group $P < GL_3(\mathbb{Z})$ and $Y(y_1, \dots y_r) \in \mathcal{Y}_\sigma$ be
a family of matrices satisfying conditions 1)-3), written in the form (\ref{eqn:Y_sum_Yi})
for a fixed automorphism $\sigma \in Aut(P)$. Writing down explicit forms of $Y$ for every $\sigma$ for every
crystallographic group $G$ we get that either
\begin{itemize}
  \item (case 1) $Y$ is a block-triangular matrix;
  \item (case 2) $\tr(Y) = \tau(Y) = 0$.
\end{itemize}
The full list of possible matrices $X \in \mathcal{X}_\sigma$ satisfying conditions 1)-2) for every point group
one can find in the Appendix \ref{app:all_norm_dim3}. The full list of $Y \in \mathcal{Y}_\sigma$ is omitted due to the
large variety\footnotemark[1]{}.

\footnotetext[1]{All the source code is provided on the author's Github: \href{https://github.com/davendiy/cryst-automata/}{https://github.com/davendiy/cryst-automata/}}

The second case is simplier. Since $\tau(Y)=\tr(Y)=0$, the characteristic polynomial of $Y^{-1}$ becomes depressed:
\[
  \chi_{Y^{-1}}(x) = x^3 + 1 / \det(Y)
\]
and it is not divisible by a monic polynomial with integral coefficients for $|\det(Y)| > 1$. As a result, the set
of self-covering degrees equals to the set of self-replicating degrees:
\[
   SCD = SRD =\{ |f_\sigma(y_1,\ldots, y_r)| > 1 : y_i\in\mathbb{Z} \}
\]
where $f_\sigma = \det(Y)$. The full list of $f_\sigma$ falling into this case are provided in the Appendix \ref{app:space_srdegrees},
Table \ref{tab:case2_srdegrees0}.

Now consider closer the first case. Without loss of generality, the matrix $Y$ has the following form:
$$
Y = \begin{pmatrix}
  C & c \\
  0 & p
\end{pmatrix},
\quad
Y^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix}
  C^{-1} & d \\
  0      & \frac{1}{p}
\end{pmatrix}
$$
so the determinant is the product of a linear combination $p(y_1, \dots y_r)$
and a quadratic polynomial $q(y_1, \dots y_r) = \det(C)$. We can also explicitly write the characteristic polynomial
of the $Y^{-1}$:
$$
  \chi_{Y^{-1}}(x) = \chi_{C^{-1}}(x)\left(x - \frac{1}{p}\right) =
  \left(x^2 - \frac{\tr(C)}{\det(C)}x + \frac{1}{\det(C)}\right)\left(x - \frac{1}{p}\right)
$$
Note that this expression also depends on the variables $y_1, \dots y_r$.
Now we can exploit the Proposition \ref{propos:planar-srdegrees} and write the implicit form of $SRD$
denoting $g_\sigma(y_1, \dots y_r) := \tr(C)$:
\begin{align*}
  SRD = \big\{ \, |(p_\sigma*q_\sigma)(y_1, \dots y_r)|  > 1 \ :  \ p_\sigma(y_1, \dots y_r) &\neq  \pm 1 \\
        (q_\sigma \pm g_\sigma)(y_1, \dots y_r) &\neq -1, \,  y_i \in \mathbb{Z} \, \big\}
\end{align*}

Similarly to the planar case, $(q_\sigma \pm g_\sigma) \neq -1$ mostly is a quadratic diophantine equation
of two variables and can be solved automatically. However, there are 4 unique special matrices that should be assessed
separately and are out of scope of this article. The first two are variations of the corresponding plane groups p1 and p2:
$$X_1 = \left(\begin{array}{rrr}
z_{0} & 0 & z_{1} \\
0 & z_{2} & 0 \\
z_{3} & 0 & z_{4}
\end{array}\right)
\quad
X_2 = \left(\begin{array}{rrr}
z_{0} & 0 & 2 \, z_{1} \\
z_{2} & z_{0} + 2 \, z_{2} & -z_{1} \\
z_{3} & 0 & z_{4}
\end{array}\right).
$$
The $X_1$ appears as is in the symmorphic groups No. 3, 6, 10, and with some variations in No. 4, 7, 11, 13, 14 after
substituting solutions for the cocycle compatibility.
Likewise, the $X_2$ appears as is in the symmorphic groups No. 5, 8, 12, and with some variations in No. 9, 15.

The remaining two contain minors that did not appear in the planar case.
$$
X_3 = \left(\begin{array}{rrr}
z_{0} & 0 & 0 \\
z_{0} + 2 \, z_{1} & z_{0} + 2 \, z_{1} & 2 \, z_{0} + 4 \, z_{1} \\
-z_{0} - z_{1} & z_{2} & -z_{0} - 2 \, z_{1}
\end{array}\right)
\quad
X_4 = \left(\begin{array}{rrr}
z_{0} & 0 & 0 \\
z_{1} & 0 & z_{0} + 2 \, z_{1} \\
z_{2} & z_{0} + 2 \, z_{2} & 0
\end{array}\right)
$$
The $X_3$ has $q_\sigma = -{\left(z_{0} + 2 \, z_{1}\right)}^{2} - 2 \, {\left(z_{0} + 2 \, z_{1}\right)} z_{2} $, it
appears in the symmorphic groups No. 22, 69 and with some variations in the group No. 70.
The $X_4$ has $q_\sigma = -{\left(z_{0} + 2 \, z_{1}\right)} {\left(z_{0} + 2 \, z_{2}\right)}$,
it appears in the symmorphic groups No. 23, 71, and with some variations in the groups No. 24, 72, 73, 74. Both have
the $q_\sigma$ that is a quadratic polynomial of three variables.

\section{Examples}
\subsection{Planar Group 7 p2mg}

The Group 7 ($p2mg$) from ITA is generated by the following elements:
$$G_7 = \left\langle\left(\begin{array}{rr|r}
1 & 0 & 1 \\
0 & 1 & 0 \\
\end{array}\right),\left(\begin{array}{rr|r}
-1 & 0 & 1/2 \\
0 & 1 & 0 \\
\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}{rr|r}
-1 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & -1 & 0 \\
\end{array}\right)  \right\rangle = \langle a,b,c \rangle$$
The basis of the matrices $\mathcal{X}$ satisfying the condition 1) consists of:
$$A_1, A_2 = \left(\begin{array}{rr}
0 & x_{1} \\
x_{0} & 0
\end{array}\right), \left(\begin{array}{rr}
x_{0} & 0 \\
0 & x_{1}
\end{array} \right) \quad   \,  x_0,x_1 \in \mathbb{Q}^*$$

Solving the system of congruences from the cocycle compatibility condition 3) we obtain that for $A_1$ system
does not have solutions. For the $A_2$ we get that $x_0 = 2y_0 + 1, x_1 = y_1, \, y_0,y_1 \in \mathbb{Z}$.
Therefore, $$SCD = \{ |\det(A_2)| = |(2y_0 + 1) y_1|, \, |\,  y_0, y_1 \in \mathbb{Z}\} = \mathbb{N}$$
The inverse $A_2^{-1}$ fits the condition 3) if and only if $|2y_0+1| > 0, |y_1| > 1$. Therefore,
$$SRD = \{ (2k + 1)(m+1) \, | \, k,m \in \mathbb{N}\}$$
or not primes and not powers of 2. In particular, the minimal self-replicating degree is 6 when $y_0 = 1, y_1 = 2$.

\subsection{Planar Group 13 p3}

The Group 13 (p3) from ITA is generated by the following elements:
$$G_{13} = \left\langle\left(\begin{array}{rrr}
0 & -1 & 0 \\
1 & -1 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{array}\right), \left(\begin{array}{rrr}
1 & 0 & 1 \\
0 & 1 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{array}\right), \left(\begin{array}{rrr}
1 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 1 & 1 \\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{array}\right)\right\rangle$$
The basis of the matrices $\mathcal{X}$ satisfying the condition 1) consists of:
$$
A_1, A_2 = \left(\begin{array}{rr}
-x_{0} & x_{0} + x_{1} \\
x_{1} & x_{0}
\end{array}\right), \left(\begin{array}{rr}
x_{0} + x_{1} & -x_{0} \\
x_{0} & x_{1}
\end{array}\right), \quad x_0,x_1 \in \mathbb{Z}
$$
The group $G_{13}$ is symmorphic, i.e. $G_{13} = P \ltimes \mathbb{Z}^2$, from which follows that congruences from 3)
are trivial.
$$
SCD = \{ |\det(A_1)|, |\det(A_2)|\} = \{ |x_{0}^{2} + {\left(x_{0} + x_{1}\right)x_1}| > 1 \, : \, x_0, x_1 \in \mathbb{Z}\}
$$

The simplicity of the virtual endomorphism corresponding to $A_1$ by Proposition \ref{propos:planar-srdegrees}
depends on the following diophantine equations:
\begin{align*}
x_{0}^{2} + {\left(x_{0} + x_{1}\right)} x_{1} & \neq \pm 1 \\
x_{0}^{2} + {\left(x_{0} + 2\right)} x_{1} + x_{1}^{2} + x_{0} & \neq -1 \\
x_{0}^{2} + {\left(x_{0} - 2\right)} x_{1} + x_{1}^{2} - x_{0} & \neq -1.
\end{align*}
The solution is a finite set of pairs
$$
(x_0, x_1) \notin
\left\{\left(0, 1\right), \left(-1, 1\right), \left(1, -1\right), \left(-1, 0\right), \left(1, 0\right), \left(0, -1\right)\right\}.
$$

Likewise, the simplicity of $A_2$ depends on the following equations:
\begin{align*}
-{\left(x_{0} + x_{1}\right)} x_{0} - x_{1}^{2} & \neq \pm 1 \\
-x_{0}^{2} - x_{0} x_{1} - x_{1}^{2} & \neq -1 \\
-x_{0}^{2} - x_{0} x_{1} - x_{1}^{2} & \neq -1
\end{align*}
and the solution is again a finite set of pairs
$$
(x_0, x_1) \notin
\left\{\left(0, 1\right), \left(-1, 1\right), \left(1, -1\right), \left(-1, 0\right), \left(1, 0\right), \left(0, -1\right)\right\}.
$$

As a result, we get the description of self-replicating degrees as follows:
\begin{align*}
SRD =  \big\{  |x_0^2 + \left(x_0 + x_1\right)x_1| > 1 \, :  \, x_0, x_1 & \in \mathbb{Z}, \\
 (x_0, x_1) & \notin [(0, 1), (-1, 1), (1, -1), (-1, 0), (1, 0), (0, -1)] \big\}
\end{align*}
